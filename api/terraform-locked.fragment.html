04 Mapping the system
41 Configuration routes reviewed

I reviewed 41 configuration routes across Terraform to identify recurring read-only states and determine where a consistent interaction pattern could scale across the product. The audit is what turned this from a page-level question into a product-level one.

05 Defining the pattern

Across the 41 routes, the same few questions came up again and again. Grouping them turned a long list of pages into a short list of decisions I could design once and reuse.

01

What happens to the selected value?

It has to stay the clearest thing on the page.
02

Should unselected options remain visible?

They explain the range a setting can take.
03

When should helper text remain?

Useful when the concept is unfamiliar or changing.
04

How should unavailable actions appear?

Present enough to be understood, not clickable.
05

How should dependent settings behave?

A read-only value can still be driven by another.
06 Research

Before choosing a direction, I worked with the team on research into what people actually need from a configuration they can't change. I helped prepare the questions, co-facilitated sessions, and synthesized what came back.

What we asked
What information do users need beyond the current value?
Are descriptions and helper text useful in read-only views?
Does seeing other available options help explain a configuration?
Can users complete review and troubleshooting work without edit access?
How does read-only fit into workflows people already have?
What we learned

Explanatory context mattered when people ran into unfamiliar or changing concepts. Some of the supporting information I assumed a read-only view could drop was doing work.

07 Early explorations

Based on what I learned from research, I explored two approaches to read-only configurations. Version M kept the page minimal and focused on the current configuration, while Version H kept helper text visible to provide more context.

M Minimal

Focused on the current configuration with less supporting information on the page.

H Helper text

Kept descriptions visible so the setting came with additional context.

08 Testing the directions

I tested both directions to see whether removing supporting information made configurations easier to review or took away context users needed. Rather than looking only at preference, I focused on whether users could understand the settings, find enough information to complete their review, and feel confident in their assessment.

09 Combining what worked

Testing gave me a clearer idea of what each direction was doing well. I explored a combined approach that kept the current configuration easy to find while preserving context where it helped users understand the setting. This exploration was not released.

Current state first

The saved configuration gets the strongest visual emphasis.

Context when it helps

Descriptions remain when they add useful information.

Less visual competition

Supporting content stays available without competing with the current state.

10 Refining the details

Small changes, big clarity.

Once the larger read-only pattern was established, I refined smaller details that helped users understand what they were seeing without adding unnecessary controls.

01Unselected options

Keep options visible even when they aren’t selected, so someone reviewing the configuration can understand what alternatives exist.

02Helper text

Keep supporting explanations and contextual information available in the read-only experience.

03Dependent settings

Preserve relationships between settings so unavailable dependent options still make sense in context.

11 Workflow

Making the move from reviewing to editing explicit.

Read-only wasn't always the end of the workflow. Someone reviewing a policy set sometimes needed to make a permitted change, then come back and confirm the result.

01View

02Edit

Choosing Edit deliberately exposes the controls needed to make a change.

03Verify

After saving, return to the view to review the resulting state.

14 What I learned

This project changed how I think about designing for complex products. I started out thinking a clearer read-only experience might mean showing less, but research showed me that some of the information I wanted to remove was helping users understand what they were looking at. Working across 41 routes also pushed me to stop thinking about individual screens and start thinking about the rules that needed to hold up across the product.
