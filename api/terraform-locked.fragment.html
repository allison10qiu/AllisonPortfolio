System audit

I audited all 41 configuration routes.

I went through every route looking at how editing worked: recurring controls, dependencies, empty states, descriptions, and anything else that would need consistent read-only behavior.

The same questions kept coming up on very different screens. That pushed me toward defining shared read-only behaviors instead of solving each screen separately.

What kept coming up across the routes
  1. 01What happens to the selected value?It has to stay the clearest thing on the page.
  2. 02Should unselected options remain visible?They explain the range a setting can take.
  3. 03When should helper text remain?Useful when the concept is unfamiliar or changing.

Research

Read-only still needed context.

Before picking a direction, we ran research on what people needed from a configuration they couldn’t change. I helped prepare the questions, co-facilitated the sessions, and wrote up the findings.

Multi-segment research · 10+ participants, internal and external

01

Current values weren’t always enough

Showing only the saved value could make unfamiliar settings difficult to interpret. Users sometimes needed the surrounding context to understand what they were looking at.

02

Descriptions still mattered

Helper text and descriptions were useful when a setting wasn’t self-explanatory. Removing them made some read-only states cleaner visually, but harder to understand.

03

Dependencies had to stay visible

Some settings were driven by or related to other settings. Those relationships still mattered in read-only mode because they explained why a configuration looked the way it did.

Exploration · not released during my internship

I combined what worked from both.

Testing showed a trade-off. M made the saved configuration easier to find, while H kept context that helped people understand unfamiliar settings. Based on that, I tried a combined direction that pulls from both. It was an exploration and wasn’t released during my internship.

Decision 01Principle 1

Selected values carry the weight. Alternatives stay quiet.

1Current value carries the weight

2Alternatives remain as context

Evidence Showing only the saved value made some settings harder to interpret. Showing every option at equal weight made the current configuration harder to scan.

Design response The selected value became the strongest visual anchor. Unselected options stayed visible when they helped explain the range of possible values, but moved into the background.

What I gave up A shorter values-only summary. The read-only page stays closer to the length of the editable one.

Decision 02Principle 3

Dependent settings stay attached to what controls them.

1 2

1Controlling setting

2Settings that depend on it, indented beneath

Evidence Some values only make sense because of a setting above them. Without that link, the read-only page was accurate but hard to follow.

Design response I kept dependent settings grouped under the setting that drives them, so a reviewer can see why a value exists even without edit access.

Decision 03Principle 2

Descriptions stay when they answer a question.

I didn’t want every description to survive just because it was on the editable page. I kept one when the setting was unfamiliar or the value couldn’t explain itself.

KeepThe description explains something the value alone cannot. RemoveThe value already communicates enough on its own.

Reflection

Showing less wasn’t always clearer.

01

Cutting context backfired

I started the project thinking a clearer read-only experience might mean showing less. Research showed me that some of the information I wanted to remove was exactly what helped people understand what they were looking at.

02

Think in rules, not pages

Working across 41 routes also pushed me to stop thinking about individual screens and start thinking about the rules that needed to hold up across the product.
