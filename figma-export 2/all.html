Making read-only access clearer in Terraform

During my Product Design internship at IBM, I worked on Terraform’s read-only experience for users who could review configurations but didn’t have permission to change them.

Removing the Edit button was the easy part. Across the product, that also removed context people needed to understand what was configured.

The problem

Read-only turned out to be more than removing the edit button.

Terraform’s role-based access control decides who can view a setting and who can change it. Making a setting read-only raised a harder question: what should stay visible once the controls are gone?

Edit Read-only
Value Current configurationSelected value Context i depends on the value above Editing SaveCancel Removed in read-only
EditableCurrent value + Context + Editing controls Read-onlyCurrent value + Necessary context − Editing controls

Removing editing controls was straightforward. Deciding what information still helped someone understand the configuration was the harder part.

01 The problem

No one could tell when a driver actually needed support.

Activity and payment data lived across Odoo, manual spreadsheets, and GPS and Yango APIs that didn't talk to each other. So a quiet driver could be underperforming, dealing with a broken bike, or unwilling to pay — and admins couldn't tell which.

01

Admins and CSMs had no easy way to see how a driver was doing — driving regularly, offline for stretches, keeping up with payments, or falling behind.

02

Checking on one driver meant opening several disconnected systems.

03

There was no clear way to decide which drivers needed help most urgently.

Afonso Ndalu Customer Success Manager

Role Customer Success Manager
Systems he checks Odoo, GPS, Yango, spreadsheets

What that meant for the roadmap Two things had to be true before anything else: the activity and payment data had to be reliable, and a flagged driver had to come with a reason. That's why explainability went into P0 and the ranked Priority Queue into P1.

04 The data problem

Before we could design the dashboards, we had to know which data we could rely on.

Driver activity and payment data came from four places. How reliable each one was, and when, set the order of the roadmap.

Odoo System of record Where driver and payment records lived.
Spreadsheets Manual Filled in by hand, so gaps and errors were possible.
GPS API Activity Vehicle movement, speed, and engine status.
Yango API Activity Trip and earnings activity from the ride-hailing side.
Why this set the order

The screens were only going to be useful if the activity and payment data behind them held up, so I worked with our tech lead on the data model before we scoped any views. The data and the performance logic came first.

05 Roadmap

I wrote the PRD, then split the product into what we'd build first, next, and not yet.

I worked with our tech lead to define the data we needed, then set priorities around it. Each tier below has the reasoning, and the trade-off where we left something out.

P0 Ship first

Authentication and the core Driver Performance Overview — nothing else mattered if admins couldn't trust the data.

If the platform flagged a driver, admins needed to see which activity or payment signals contributed. We treated explainability as a requirement from the start.

Admin and CSM dashboards focused on the same two things: real activity and payment status.

P1 Once the data held up

The Priority Queue view and risk scoring, giving CSMs a clear read on who to check on first.

Trade-off Held predictive risk scoring until the system could explain itself — flagging a driver without a reason wasn't good enough.
P2 Deferred
Trade-off The driver-facing mobile dashboard and rewards, since admins and CSMs needed visibility first.
Trade-off CSM messaging and CSM performance scoring, so CSMs had support tools before messaging tools.
06 Decision — explainability as P0
Product question

What does someone need to see to understand a driver's score?

My decision

I put explainability in the P0 requirements, so an admin could see which activity and payment signals went into a driver's score.

Team output — designed by Tiffany Liu and Isabella Raffo
Anda
The breakdown shows the weight behind each factor: payment timeliness, hours worked, collection rate, portfolio risk.
Collection Rate and % Portfolio at Risk are shown as ratios, so an admin can check the math.

07 Decision — holding predictive scoring

Predictive scoring stayed in P1 until we could explain the results.

Anda
Designed by Tiffany Liu and Isabella Raffo
Why it was valuable

Risk scoring would give CSMs a clear read on who to check on first, instead of working through their drivers one by one.

Why it wasn't P0

I kept predictive scoring in P1 because we couldn't show a driver as high risk without being able to explain why.

08 Decision — working with synthetic data
The dependency

Access to real driver data took longer than expected, and the timeline wasn't ours to set.

My decision

We used synthetic activity and payment patterns so design and development could keep moving while we waited.

What it didn't fix

Synthetic patterns kept design and development moving, but they didn't prove the scoring would work correctly with real drivers.

10 Outcome & reflection

This was my first time owning the product roadmap instead of contributing to one part of a project.

What the team got done

By the end of the semester the team had designed the P0 work — authentication, the driver performance overview with its score breakdown, and the admin and CSM dashboards — plus the P1 Priority Queue. The driver-facing app, rewards, and CSM messaging stayed out of scope on purpose. The interfaces were designed by Tiffany Liu and Isabella Raffo from the requirements I wrote.

What I learned

How much the product decisions depended on the data. The screens only worked if the data and the model behind them held up, so most of my time went into whether that part was on track. I also got more comfortable asking our tech lead questions until I actually understood what we were building, instead of nodding along.

Prioritizing meant deciding what we wouldn't build. Putting the core driver data first meant the driver-facing app and CSM messaging waited, and planning around data access I couldn't control is a big part of what I took from the semester.

What still needed validation Synthetic data kept design and development moving while access to real driver data was delayed, but it never showed that the scoring would behave correctly on real drivers. That was still open when the semester ended.

OSE was managing hundreds of cases through email and spreadsheets.

For a small team, keeping up with that information took time away from supporting survivors.

02

Problem statement

OSE needed a secure way for admins and volunteers to understand a case quickly, keep its history in one place, and hand work off without losing context.

Robert Gomez OSE volunteer

03 Considerations

Safety shaped every part of the workflow.

Privacy above all

FLOW handles sensitive information about people seeking help, so privacy and safety had to come before convenience.

An ongoing process

A case can move between survivors, admins, and volunteers over time. The workflow needed to preserve enough history that someone new could understand what had already happened.

A lot of information per case

Each case could contain a lot of information. I focused on making the most important details easy to find first, with deeper information available when someone needed it.

04 Design system

We needed a system the whole platform could share.

OSE didn't have a full product design system when we started, so we built the components FLOW needed around its existing logo and typography. Establishing those patterns early helped the different parts of the platform feel like the same product.

06 Outcome & reflection

What we handed over, and what I took from it.

Delivered work

The flows I designed inside FLOW: the survivor case dashboard, the case card, secure messaging, the form builder, and the mobile-first intake.

OSE doesn't have developers on staff, so the form builder mattered as much as the screens did. It's how their team keeps forms up to date after handoff.

Reflection
Most of my earlier projects were about making a task faster. Here privacy and safety came before convenience, and that changed which options were even on the table.
A case moves between survivors, admins, and volunteers, so keeping its history attached to it was part of the design. Whoever picks it up next needs to know what already happened.
Sortable lists and CSV exports weren't the exciting parts, but they replaced work OSE was doing by hand. I pay more attention now to whether a feature actually takes work off someone's plate.

03 Considerations

Most of the challenge was figuring out how the data should work.

Organizing the data first

Before designing the visualization flow, I spent time understanding how NABU's data was organized and how an admin would need to move through it.

Designing around tables

A lot of this product is tables rather than typical consumer UI, so I had to know which fields mattered to an admin before deciding how a row should be laid out.

Three months to production

We had one semester to design and ship, so we focused on the workflows NABU needed most instead of trying to solve everything.

NABU's system came with the project. We added what the platform needed.

NABU gave us colors, typography, and branding guidelines, so I didn't build the system from scratch. What we added were the table, form, and button patterns the platform needed, which kept more of our time on the data and workflow problems.

05 Final product

A lot of this project came down to tables: how much to put in a row, and what happens when there are two hundred of them.

These are the three decisions I'd talk through first.

01 Decision 01

Put the assigned visualizations inside the table row

NABU

A role decides which data a partner can see, so the table doesn’t help much if you can’t tell what each role gives access to. There are 200 roles, and the descriptions run long.

A dropdown inside the row expands the assigned visualizations, so several can be read without widening the table
Edit and delete sit at the end of each row, next to Create New Role above the table
Search and pagination bracket the table, since scanning all 200 was never realistic
Trade-off An open dropdown covers the rows beneath it, so only one role can be expanded at a time. Comparing two roles side by side came up less often than checking one, so we kept it.
02 Decision 02

Kept adding a partner in a modal instead of a separate page

NABU

Partner requests used to be handled one at a time in different places. Putting them in one table only helps if acting on one doesn’t send the admin off somewhere else.

The modal shows the roles that already exist, so nobody has to open another tab to look them up
Multi-select lets an admin approve or reject several requests at once
Search finds one requester in a longer list
03 Decision 03

Made a role the single place access is decided

NABU

Creating a role means naming it, describing it, and picking the visualizations it can reach. That chain is the whole access model: the role decides the assigned visualizations, and those decide what a partner sees. It made sense to keep all of it in one form.

Creating, editing, and deleting all happen where the roles are listed
An admin can give a partner what they need without exposing the rest of the data

06 Takeaways

Where it landed, and what it changed about how I design.

Where it landed

The part of the platform we designed put four jobs in one place: approving partners, managing roles and what each one can reach, organizing the data behind them, and building the visualizations NABU shares. I don't have usage data from after handoff, so I can't say how their week looks now.

04 Design decisions

The five decisions I’d point to first.

The final product is a mobile web app where parents set habit goals for each child, get reminders to keep up with them, and stay connected to Brilliant Cities between programs.

  1. Decision 01

    Gave each goal category its own card instead of a plain list

    Why

    Brilliant Cities was specific about the categories they wanted, but the names alone didn’t tell a parent which habits were in each one. Cards let us show that.

    Trade-off

    Cards take up more room than a list on a phone. We added multi-select checkboxes so parents could pick a few categories at once instead of scrolling through all of them.

  2. Decision 02

    Used filter pills instead of a search bar

    Why

    We took inspiration from shopping site filters. Typing means remembering the exact category name, and the icons and colors on the pills let parents recognize categories instead.

    Trade-off

    Pills take up more space than a search bar. We thought that was worth it since parents don’t have to type or remember anything.

  3. Decision 03

    Grouped search results by category so parents could swipe through them

    Why

    Splitting habits up by category lets a parent see all the options in one area before moving on, which made the most sense for a mobile site.

    Trade-off

    Unlike a typical search, parents can only browse the options Brilliant Cities set. Someone used to typing a query has to scan instead.

  4. Decision 04

    Laid out the habit tips as steps, grouped by age

    Why

    We had wording the psychologist approved but no format for it. Steps help parents figure out the interface the first time, and grouping by age gets them to habits that fit their kid.

    Trade-off

    Grouping by age means a parent with kids in different age ranges has to look at more than one group.

  5. Decision 05

    Gave each child their own page, with a floating button to add more

    Why

    Parents can add as many habits as they want, so a page per child keeps each list from getting crowded. Grouping by goal also makes it easier to see progress in one area.

    Trade-off

    Switching between kids takes an extra tap, but each list stays short, which matters more on a phone.

05 Design system

We built the design system before we designed screens.

Brilliant Cities gave us a branding kit — colors, fonts, and a few components like accordions — but not a full component library. I worked with another product designer to build the rest of what our screens needed: forms, tags, buttons, and more. Having it settled early meant our screens matched even though we were splitting them between us.

We collaborated on most of the components. The filter pill search was mine — I designed the pills and how they behave, and that pattern ended up carrying the whole search flow.

06 Takeaways

What I took away from this project.

  1. 01

    Working with another designer takes real communication

    This was my first time designing alongside another designer. We spent a lot of time agreeing on what we were making, and learning how the other person works. That mattered as much as the screens.

  2. 02

    Running a project end to end on a tight timeline

    It was also my first time managing a project from start to finish with a real deadline. It pushed me to design faster and to be honest with myself about what actually mattered.

  3. 03

    What I wanted to design wasn’t always what we could ship

    I’d only done solo projects before this. Working with a PM, developers, and a child psychologist meant weighing what I wanted to design against what we could actually build in the semester.
