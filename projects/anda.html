01The problem

No one could tell when a driver actually needed support.

Activity and payment data lived across Odoo, manual spreadsheets, and GPS and Yango APIs that didn’t talk to each other. So an inactive driver could be underperforming, dealing with a vehicle issue, or falling behind on payments — and admins couldn’t tell which.

  1. 01

    Admins and CSMs had no easy way to see how a driver was doing — driving regularly, offline for stretches, keeping up with payments, or falling behind.

  2. 02

    Checking on one driver meant opening several disconnected systems.

  3. 03

    There was no clear way to decide which drivers needed help most urgently.

02Who we designed for

The Customer Success Managers who had to decide who needed help first.

CSMs needed to know whether a driver needed intervention before reaching out — and understand why the system had flagged them.
What he needs

A ranked view showing who needs help most urgently and why, grounded in activity and payment signals he can explain to a driver.

What that meant for the roadmap

Two things had to be true before anything else: the activity and payment data had to be reliable, and a flagged driver had to come with a reason. That’s why explainability went into P0 and the ranked Priority Queue into P1.

04The data problem

Before we could design the dashboards, we had to know which data we could rely on.

Driver activity and payment data came from four places. Their reliability and availability shaped what we could prioritize first.

System of recordOdoo

Where driver and payment records lived.

ManualSpreadsheets

Filled in by hand, so gaps and errors were possible.

ActivityGPS API

Vehicle movement, speed, and engine status.

ActivityYango API

Trip and earnings activity from the ride-hailing side.

Why this set the order

The screens were only going to be useful if the activity and payment data behind them held up, so I worked with our tech lead on the data model before we scoped any views. Reliable data and interpretable performance logic had to come before the views built on top of them.

05Roadmap

I wrote the PRD, then split the product into what we’d build first, next, and not yet.

I worked with our tech lead to define the data we needed, then prioritized the product around those dependencies. Each tier reflects what we needed first and what we intentionally deferred.

P0Ship first

Authentication and the core Driver Performance Overview — nothing else mattered if admins couldn’t trust the data.

Admin and CSM dashboards focused on the same two things: real activity and payment status.

P1Once the data held up

The Priority Queue view and risk scoring, giving CSMs a clear read on who to check on first.

Trade-off

Held predictive risk scoring until the system could explain itself — flagging a driver without a reason wasn’t good enough.

P2Deferred
Trade-off

The driver-facing mobile dashboard and rewards, since admins and CSMs needed visibility first.

Trade-off

CSM messaging and CSM performance scoring, so CSMs had support tools before messaging tools.

We treated explainability as a requirement from the start.

If the platform flagged a driver, admins needed to see which activity or payment signals contributed.

Decision — explainability as P0

06Decision — explainability as P0
Product question

What does someone need to see to understand a driver’s score?

My decision

I made explainability a P0 requirement: admins needed to see which activity and payment signals contributed to a driver’s score.

  1. 01

    The breakdown shows the weight behind each factor: payment timeliness, hours worked, collection rate, portfolio risk.

  2. 02

    Collection Rate and % Portfolio at Risk are shown as ratios, so an admin can check the math.

  3. 03

    An admin can point to the reason a driver was flagged, which is what the requirements asked for.

07Decision — holding predictive scoring

Predictive scoring stayed in P1 until we could explain the results.

Why it was valuable

Risk scoring would give CSMs a clear read on who to check on first, instead of working through their drivers one by one.

Why it wasn’t P0

A risk label without a clear reason wouldn’t give CSMs enough information to understand or act on it.

Decision — working with synthetic data

08Decision — working with synthetic data
  1. The dependency→

    Access to real driver data was delayed beyond our team’s control.

  2. My decision→

    I chose to move forward with synthetic activity and payment patterns so design and development could keep moving while we waited.

  3. What it didn’t fix

    Synthetic data kept the team moving, but it did not validate that the scoring would behave correctly with real drivers.

10Outcome & reflection

This was my first time owning the product roadmap instead of contributing to one part of a project.

What the team got done

By the end of the semester, the team had designed the P0 experience — authentication, driver performance and score breakdowns, and the admin and CSM dashboards — along with the P1 Priority Queue. The driver-facing app, rewards, and CSM messaging remained intentionally out of scope. Tiffany Liu and Isabella Raffo designed the interfaces from the requirements and priorities I defined.

What I learned
01
Product decisions depended on the data.

I learned that I couldn’t make useful roadmap decisions without understanding the data and logic underneath the interface. Working closely with our tech lead made me more comfortable asking technical questions until I understood the constraints well enough to make the call.

02
Prioritization meant choosing what not to build.

Putting reliable driver data first meant delaying the driver-facing app and CSM messaging. I also learned to separate the work we could keep moving from dependencies, like real data access, that the team couldn’t control.

What still needed validation

Synthetic data kept design and development moving while access to real driver data was delayed, but it never showed that the scoring would behave correctly on real drivers. That was still open when the semester ended.
