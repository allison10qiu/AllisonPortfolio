Designing a habit tracker Brilliant Cities families can use at home

Brilliant Cities runs in-person programs for families through neighborhood hubs. Through Hack4Impact, I helped design a mobile web app where parents set healthy-habit goals for each child and keep up with them between programs.

Role
Product Designer
Team
Hack4Impact, for Brilliant Cities
Timeline
Sept–Dec 2024
Focus
  • Product design

Problem
Staff usually checked in with families about twice a year, so support tapered off once a program ended and Brilliant Cities had little visibility into how families were doing at home.
My contribution
I designed how parents browse habits by category and worked with another designer on the parent flows and shared components.

Context

Support tapered off once families went home

Brilliant Cities connects with families through community dinners, fitness classes, computer lab hours, and other programs at neighborhood hubs. After that, staff told us they typically checked in about twice a year, leaving a long stretch with little visibility into how things were going at home.

Families also had different levels of comfort with technology, so the main actions needed to make sense without much onboarding.

Cynthia Grey

Composite persona

Single mom of two, ages 6 and 9, who works full time and rarely makes it to programs in person.

What she needs
Simple things she can do at home, and a way to see whether they’re sticking.

Constraints

Every screen had to make sense the first time

Designing for first use

Families would usually reach the tracker through Brilliant Cities’ website on a phone, and they brought different levels of comfort with technology. We needed parents to be able to find a goal and get started with very little onboarding.

Habit guidance

A child psychologist reviewed the wording of the habit guidance. We then had to decide how to present that guidance so parents could find suggestions that fit their child’s age.

Shifting scope, one semester

Scope changed during the project, so we stayed flexible while still designing and shipping within one semester.

Key decisions

Helping parents find habits and keep up with them

01

Showing what’s inside each goal category

Brilliant Cities was specific about the goal categories it wanted, but the names alone didn’t tell a parent which habits were inside. Cards let us show that, and multi-select lets parents choose several categories at once instead of repeating the flow.

02

Helping parents browse habits by category

I used category filters so parents could recognize the kind of habit they wanted without having to remember what to type. The filters took up more room than a search field and limited browsing to the categories we provided, which was a trade-off I accepted to make the available choices visible from the start.

  1. Filter pills in place of a search bar.

  2. After filtering, results are grouped by category.

03

Turning approved tips into steps, grouped by age

We had wording the psychologist approved but no format for it. Steps help parents understand the interface the first time, and grouping by age gets them to habits that fit their kid, though a parent with kids in different age ranges has to check more than one group.

04

Giving each child their own page

Parents can add as many habits as they want, so a page per child keeps each list short. A floating button adds more, and grouping by goal makes progress in one area easier to see. Switching between kids takes an extra tap, which we accepted to keep each list readable on a phone.

Outcome

What we handed off

The mobile web app gave parents a place to set habit goals for each child and keep up with them between programs. I designed the category filter and co-designed the parent flows and shared components, which helped the two of us work on different screens without making the experience feel disconnected.

What I took from the project

This was my first time designing alongside another designer, and I learned how much easier it was to work independently once we’d agreed on the components we wanted to share. That mattered because we were designing different parts of the same experience, and a parent moving between them still needed to recognize how things worked. Collaborating with our PM, developers, and the child psychologist also taught me to consider what the team could build in a semester and what Brilliant Cities could maintain afterward.
