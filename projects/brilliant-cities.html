brilliant cities

A habit tracker families can keep using at home

I helped design a mobile habit tracker where parents can set goals for each of their kids and keep up with them at home. We worked with Brilliant Cities over three months to extend the support families get from their in-person programs.

01 Problem

Brilliant Cities mostly connects with families during in-person programs, so support tapered off once families went home.

  1. 01

    What they run

    In-person family programming

    Brilliant Cities runs community dinners, fitness classes, computer lab hours, and other programs out of neighborhood hubs.

    Dozens of program offerings

  2. 02

    What happens after

    Limited availability for follow-ups

    Staff told us they typically check in with families about twice a year, leaving a long stretch with little visibility into how things are going at home.

    Check-ins about twice a year

  3. 03

    What that means for us

    Different levels of tech comfort

    Brilliant Cities told us families had different levels of comfort with technology, so we tried to make the main actions understandable without much onboarding.

    Families vary a lot in tech comfort

Problem statement

Families didn’t have an easy way to keep building healthy habits after a program ended, and Brilliant Cities didn’t have a way to see how they were doing in between.

02 Who we designed for

We designed for parents who want to help their kids but don’t have much free time.

“I want my kids to develop healthy habits, but I don’t have time to attend the programs.”

Cynthia wants her kids to eat better and stay active, but she can’t always make it to Brilliant Cities’ programs. She’s looking for simple things she can do at home, and for a way to see whether they’re sticking.

03 Constraints

A few things we had to design around.

Most of the decisions below come back to one of these.

  1. 01

    Had to be clear on first use

    Most of our users were adults who may not use apps often, so I kept screens simple and obvious.

  2. 02

    Deliverables kept changing

    Scope wasn’t clear at the start because of miscommunication inside Brilliant Cities, so I had to stay flexible.

  3. 03

    Three months, start to finish

    We had to design and get to production in one semester, so I had to work efficiently without cutting corners.

  4. 04

    Wording reviewed by a child psychologist

    Copy mattered as much as layout.

  5. 05

    Mobile web, not an app

    The client wanted a mobile website, since families would mostly reach it through their site on a phone.

04 Design decisions

The five decisions I’d point to first.

The final product is a mobile web app where parents set habit goals for each child, get reminders to keep up with them, and stay connected to Brilliant Cities between programs.

  1. Decision 01

    Gave each goal category its own card instead of a plain list

    Why

    Brilliant Cities was specific about the categories they wanted, but the names alone didn’t tell a parent which habits were in each one. Cards let us show that.

    Trade-off

    Cards take up more room than a list on a phone. We added multi-select checkboxes so parents could pick a few categories at once instead of scrolling through all of them.

  2. Decision 02

    Used filter pills instead of a search bar

    Why

    We took inspiration from shopping site filters. Typing means remembering the exact category name, and the icons and colors on the pills let parents recognize categories instead.

    Trade-off

    Pills take up more space than a search bar. We thought that was worth it since parents don’t have to type or remember anything.

  3. Decision 03

    Grouped search results by category so parents could swipe through them

    Why

    Splitting habits up by category lets a parent see all the options in one area before moving on, which made the most sense for a mobile site.

    Trade-off

    Unlike a typical search, parents can only browse set options, which may feel less intuitive to those used to searching freely.

  4. Decision 04

    Laid out the habit tips as steps, grouped by age

    Why

    We had wording the psychologist approved but no format for it. Steps help parents figure out the interface the first time, and grouping by age gets them to habits that fit their kid.

    Trade-off

    Grouping by age means a parent with kids in different age ranges has to look at more than one group.

  5. Decision 05

    Gave each child their own page, with a floating button to add more

    Why

    Parents can add as many habits as they want, so a page per child keeps each list from getting crowded. Grouping by goal also makes it easier to see progress in one area.

    Trade-off

    Switching between kids takes an extra tap, but each list stays short, which matters more on a phone.

05 Design system

We built the design system before we designed screens.

Brilliant Cities gave us a branding kit — colors, fonts, and a few components like accordions — but not a full component library. I worked with another product designer to build the rest of what our screens needed: forms, tags, buttons, and more. Having it settled early meant our screens matched even though we were splitting them between us.

We collaborated on most of the components. The filter pill search was mine — I designed the pills and how they behave, and that pattern ended up carrying the whole search flow.

06 Takeaways

What I took away from this project.

  1. 01

    Working with another designer takes real communication

    This was my first time designing alongside another designer. We spent time getting to a shared vision and learning how each other works, and that turned out to matter as much as the design itself.

  2. 02

    Running a project end to end on a tight timeline

    It was also my first time managing a project from start to finish with a real deadline. It pushed me to design faster and to be honest with myself about what actually mattered.

  3. 03

    Balancing creativity with business and technical limits

    I’d only done solo projects before this, so working with a PM, developers, and a child psychologist taught me how to weigh what I wanted to design against what was feasible.
