Designing NABU’s tools for partner access and reporting

NABU publishes storybooks in children’s native languages for more than 12 million learners. Through Hack4Impact, I worked on the internal platform NABU uses to manage partners and share data with them.

Role

Focus
  • Data visualization

Problem
To set up each partner’s dashboard, admins had to manually pull together data from MySQL, spreadsheets, and Google Analytics.
My contribution
Working within NABU’s existing brand, I designed the tools admins use to review partner requests, manage access through roles, and build visualizations.

Context

Every new partner meant another dashboard to set up

When a partner joined, NABU’s admins gathered information from country managers and pulled data from MySQL, spreadsheets, and Google Analytics to set up that partner’s dashboard. Because they did this by hand for each partner, it took time to bring the data together and was harder to keep dashboards consistent.

What she needs
A way to approve partners, manage what they can access, and build visualizations without setting up each dashboard from scratch.

Constraints

The data shaped the interface

Understanding the data

Before I could design the builder, I needed to understand how NABU’s data was organized and what an admin would need to do with it to create a visualization.

Designing for tables

Most of the work happened in tables, so I needed to know which fields admins relied on and how partners, roles, and visualizations related to each other before deciding what a row should show.

Working within a semester

We had one semester, so we focused on the workflows NABU needed to review partner requests, manage access, and share data.

Working with NABU’s brand

NABU already had its own colors and typography, which gave us a starting point as we designed the tables, forms, and buttons the platform needed.

Key decisions

Designing roles and partner approvals

01

Defining access in the role form

Since a partner’s role determines which visualizations they can see, I kept the role’s name, description, and assigned visualizations together. That way, an admin could see what a role was for as they decided what it should give partners access to.

02

Keeping a long roles table easy to scan

With around 200 roles to manage, showing every assigned visualization at once would have made the table harder to scan. I kept each list accessible from its row so admins could find a role first and open the details when they needed them.

Trade-off

The open list covers the rows below it, and only one list can be open at a time. That works when checking a single role, though comparing two means opening them one after the other.

03

Managing partners from the table

I kept partner management in the table view, where admins could find a specific request or approve or reject several at once. When adding a partner, they could assign an existing role in the same form, which determined which published visualizations the partner could see.

The builder

Filtering data and reviewing it before publishing

The last two steps of the builder show how admins narrow down the data they want to share and check what will go into the visualization before they publish it.

How it connects

From a partner request to a published visualization

  1. Partner request

    An admin reviews and approves the request in the partners table.

  2. Role assignment

    When adding the partner, the admin assigns an existing role that determines which visualizations they can access.

  3. Visualization builder

    The admin chooses the data to include and reviews it before publishing.

  4. Published visualization

    The partner can view published visualizations that are available to their role.

Outcome

What we handed off

Our team handed off designs for partner approvals, role management, data organization, and visualization building. My work connected the steps admins take to approve partners, set their access, and prepare the visualizations NABU shares. I don’t have post-handoff usage data, so I don’t know whether the tools reduced setup time.

What I took from the project

Working on NABU made me think more carefully about how much detail belongs in a table. An admin looking through around 200 roles needed to find the right one before checking its access, so I kept the list of assigned visualizations inside the row. That kept the table easy to scan, though it made comparing roles less convenient. I’ll carry that question into other projects: what does someone need to see while they’re looking, and what can wait until they’ve found it?
